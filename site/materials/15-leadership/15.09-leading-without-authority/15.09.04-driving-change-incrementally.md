# Lesson 4: Driving Change Incrementally

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Understand why incremental change is effective for technical organizations
- Apply strategies for driving change without formal authority
- Balance incremental and revolutionary change appropriately
- Break down large changes into small, strategic steps
- Recognize and overcome resistance to change

---

## Introduction

Large, transformative changes are risky, disruptive, and often fail. Incremental change—making gradual adjustments and optimizations while emphasizing stability—is far more effective, especially when you lack formal authority to mandate change.

Staff+ engineers who master incremental change can transform organizations over time through small, strategic moves that compound into significant impact.

## Key Concepts Summary

### Why Incremental Change Works

**Challenges with big-bang transformations:**
- High risk and disruption
- Difficult to get buy-in without authority
- Hard to adapt based on learning
- Often trigger resistance and fear

**Benefits of incremental approach:**
- Lower risk per step
- Easier to secure support for small experiments
- Can adjust course based on feedback
- Builds momentum through visible wins
- Allows organization to absorb change gradually

**Key insight:** Major strategies rarely emerge from purely rational-analytical planning. Instead, they develop through a fragmented, evolutionary process where formal analysis blends with internal decisions and external events, allowing consensus to develop gradually.

### Three Strategies for Driving Change

**1. Cheerleading (Cultural Change)**
- Enthusiastically invite teams into the change
- Celebrate early wins and create visible opportunities
- Best for: Gradual cultural shifts that can be adopted optionally
- Example: Building accountability practices, improving documentation

**2. Mandating (Critical Infrastructure)**
- Establish firm requirements and deadlines
- Requires positional authority or executive sponsorship
- Best for: Critical issues affecting system integrity
- Example: Security patches, compliance requirements

**3. Systematic (Foundational Change)**
- Create infrastructure that makes the new way easier than the old way
- Reduce friction rather than forcing behavior change
- Best for: Fundamental shifts in how work gets done
- Example: Automated testing, observability platforms

**For those without authority, focus on cheerleading and systematic approaches.**

### Breaking Change into Small Steps

**Traditional approach (doesn't work well):**
```
Big Vision → Detailed Plan → Long Implementation → Results?
```

**Incremental approach (effective):**
```
Vision → Small Experiment → Learn → Adjust → Next Step → Repeat
```

**Characteristics of good incremental steps:**
- **Valuable:** Delivers real benefit even if it's the last step
- **Reversible:** Can be undone if it doesn't work
- **Visible:** Progress is clear to stakeholders
- **Aligned:** Supports the broader strategic direction
- **Small enough:** Can be completed in days or weeks, not months

### Organizational Evolution Algorithm

Will Larson's five-step approach:

1. **Measure:** Understand current performance and constraints
2. **Size:** Compare to peers and assess against goals
3. **Structure:** Organize teams appropriately for current challenges
4. **Project:** Forecast future growth and needs
5. **Rest:** Allow time for teams to develop mastery before the next change

**Critical insight:** Rest between changes. Continuous transformation without consolidation prevents mastery and creates exhaustion.

### Seven Tenets for Lasting Change

1. **Spot invisible blockers early:** Identify and address obstacles before they derail progress
2. **Empower people over process:** Focus on enabling teams, not just implementing procedures
3. **Use data to guide, not punish:** Metrics should inform decisions, not create fear
4. **Maintain stable teams:** Avoid constant reorganization during transformation
5. **Align incentives with outcomes:** Ensure reward structures support desired behaviors
6. **Activate informal networks:** Work through relationships and influence, not just hierarchy
7. **Measure meaningful metrics:** Track what actually matters, not vanity metrics

### Overcoming Resistance

**Common sources of resistance:**
- Fear of the unknown or loss of status
- Previous failed change initiatives
- Lack of understanding of "why"
- Insufficient support or resources
- Change fatigue from too many initiatives

**Strategies to address:**
- **Frame change as enabling:** Teams drive change, it doesn't happen to them
- **Make it safe to experiment:** Emphasize learning over perfection
- **Celebrate early adopters:** Create visible success stories
- **Address blockers proactively:** Remove obstacles before they grow
- **Communicate relentlessly:** Keep explaining the "why" and showing progress
- **Make the new way easier:** Reduce friction through infrastructure and tooling

## Practice Exercise

After completing the materials, design an incremental change plan (20 minutes):

1. **Choose a change you want to drive:**
   - Example: Improving test coverage, adopting better documentation practices, implementing observability

2. **Break it down into small steps:**
   - What's the smallest valuable step you could take this week?
   - What would be the next three steps after that?
   - Ensure each step delivers value independently

3. **Identify your approach:**
   - Would cheerleading, systematic, or combination work best?
   - Why?

4. **Plan for resistance:**
   - What objections might you hear?
   - How will you address them?
   - Who could be early adopters to create momentum?

5. **Define success:**
   - How will you know each step is working?
   - What metrics or signals will you track?

## Key Takeaways

- Incremental change is more effective than big transformations, especially without authority
- Use cheerleading (cultural) and systematic (infrastructure) approaches when you can't mandate
- Break change into small, valuable, visible steps that deliver benefit independently
- Frame change as something teams enable, not something happening to them
- Rest between major changes to allow teams to develop mastery
- Address invisible blockers early before they derail progress
- Create momentum through early wins and visible success stories
- Make the new way easier than the old way through infrastructure and tooling
- Measure meaningful metrics that guide decisions rather than create fear
