# Lesson 4: Handling Ambiguity and Requirements Gathering

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Navigate intentionally ambiguous interview questions
- Ask effective clarifying questions to narrow scope
- Make and validate reasonable assumptions
- Define functional and non-functional requirements systematically
- Demonstrate ownership and leadership through proactive questioning

---

## Introduction

Ambiguity is not a bug in system design interviews—it's a feature. Interviewers deliberately provide incomplete information to observe how you handle uncertainty, a reality of real-world engineering. The best candidates don't wait for complete specifications; they proactively clarify requirements, make reasonable assumptions, and validate their understanding.

This lesson teaches you how to transform vague problems into well-defined design challenges.

## Key Concepts Summary

### Why Ambiguity is Intentional

**Purpose of ambiguous questions:**
- Test your ability to handle incomplete information
- Observe how you operate without detailed specifications
- Assess your judgment about what questions to ask
- Evaluate ownership and leadership qualities

**What interviewers want to see:**
- Proactive clarification before designing
- Structured approach to gathering requirements
- Reasonable assumptions when information is unavailable
- Validation of understanding throughout

**Red flag behaviors:**
- Starting design immediately without questions
- Making wild assumptions without validation
- Getting stuck when information is missing
- Waiting passively for more details

### The Requirements Gathering Framework

**Step 1: Clarify Core Features**

Ask about the primary use cases and essential features:
- "What are the core features we need to support?"
- "Are there features we should explicitly exclude?"
- "What's the primary user flow?"
- "Should we support [specific feature], or is that out of scope?"

Example for "Design Twitter":
- "Should we support tweeting, following, and timeline viewing?"
- "Are we including direct messages, or just public tweets?"
- "Do we need to support hashtags and search?"

**Step 2: Understand Scale and Performance**

Quantify the system's expected scale:
- "How many users do we expect?"
- "What's the expected read/write ratio?"
- "What's the expected data volume over time?"
- "Do we need to support global users or specific regions?"

Example questions:
- "Are we designing for 1 million or 1 billion users?"
- "How many requests per second at peak?"
- "What's the average and maximum size of content?"

**Step 3: Define Non-Functional Requirements**

Clarify quality attributes and constraints:
- **Availability:** "What level of uptime is required? 99.9%? 99.99%?"
- **Latency:** "What's acceptable response time? Sub-second? Sub-100ms?"
- **Consistency:** "Can we accept eventual consistency, or do we need strong consistency?"
- **Durability:** "How critical is it that we never lose data?"
- **Security:** "What security and privacy requirements exist?"

**Step 4: Identify Constraints**

Ask about limitations and requirements:
- "Are there specific technologies we must use or avoid?"
- "What's the budget constraint?"
- "Are there compliance requirements (GDPR, HIPAA)?"
- "Do we need to integrate with existing systems?"

**Step 5: Prioritize Features**

When scope is large, prioritize:
- "If we can only build one feature today, which is most important?"
- "Are there must-haves vs. nice-to-haves?"
- "What would an MVP look like?"

### Making and Validating Assumptions

**When to make assumptions:**
- Information is unavailable or would take too long to clarify
- You need to move forward to make progress
- The assumption is reasonable and common

**How to make assumptions effectively:**

1. **State them explicitly:**
   - "I'm assuming we have about 100 million daily active users"
   - "I'll assume read-to-write ratio is 100:1, which is typical for social media"

2. **Validate with the interviewer:**
   - "Does that assumption sound reasonable?"
   - "Would you like me to adjust any of these numbers?"

3. **Show your reasoning:**
   - "Based on typical social media patterns, I'm estimating..."
   - "If we're targeting enterprise customers, we'd expect..."

4. **Be ready to adapt:**
   - "If the scale is actually 10x higher, we'd need to..."
   - "That changes things—let me adjust my approach..."

**Good assumptions:**
- Based on industry norms
- Clearly stated and validated
- Reasonable for the problem domain
- Flexible if wrong

**Bad assumptions:**
- Made silently without stating them
- Wildly optimistic or pessimistic
- Never validated
- Rigidly held when challenged

### Effective Clarifying Questions

**Questions about scope:**
- "Are we designing the entire system or focusing on [specific component]?"
- "Should we discuss the mobile app architecture or just the backend?"
- "Is this for the MVP or the mature system?"

**Questions about users:**
- "Who are the primary users?"
- "What devices do they use?"
- "Where are they located geographically?"

**Questions about data:**
- "What types of data does the system handle?"
- "What's the data retention policy?"
- "Do we need analytics or just core functionality?"

**Questions about integration:**
- "Does this integrate with existing systems?"
- "Do we need APIs for third-party developers?"
- "What authentication mechanisms should we support?"

**Questions about operations:**
- "Who maintains and operates this system?"
- "What monitoring and alerting is needed?"
- "How do we handle deployments and rollbacks?"

### The 7-Step Approach

Structured framework for handling any system design question:

1. **Clarify the problem** - Ask about core features, scale, and constraints
2. **Define scope collaboratively** - Propose what to focus on and discuss non-functional requirements
3. **Sketch high-level architecture** - Start broad before diving into details
4. **Apply core systems principles** - Scalability, availability, fault tolerance
5. **Communicate trade-offs and assumptions** - Explicit reasoning
6. **Explain why patterns fit** - Not just their names
7. **Show restraint** - Avoid unnecessary complexity

### Demonstrating Ownership

Strong candidates show leadership through questioning:

**Proactive clarification:**
- Don't wait to be told everything
- Take ownership of defining the problem
- Show you can operate with autonomy

**Structured thinking:**
- Organized approach to gathering requirements
- Systematic coverage of dimensions
- Clear priorities

**Strategic focus:**
- Ask high-impact questions
- Don't get lost in minor details
- Balance breadth and depth

**Collaborative validation:**
- Check understanding frequently
- Adapt based on feedback
- Build shared context

## Practice Exercise

After completing the materials, practice these scenarios (25 minutes):

**Exercise 1: Requirements Extraction (15 minutes)**

You're asked: "Design Instagram"

Draft your clarifying questions in these categories:
1. **Core Features** (5 questions)
2. **Scale and Performance** (5 questions)
3. **Non-Functional Requirements** (3 questions)
4. **Constraints** (2 questions)

For each question, think about:
- Why you're asking it
- How the answer would affect your design
- What assumption you'd make if the answer is vague

**Exercise 2: Assumption Validation (10 minutes)**

For "Design a URL Shortener," practice stating and validating assumptions:

1. Write out 5 key assumptions you'd need to make
2. For each, draft how you'd state it to the interviewer
3. Explain how you'd adapt if the assumption was wrong

Example format:
- **Assumption:** "I'm assuming 1 million new URLs per day"
- **Validation:** "Does that scale sound right, or should I adjust?"
- **Adaptation:** "If it's 100 million per day instead, we'd need sharding from day one rather than starting with a single database"

## Key Takeaways

- Ambiguity is intentional—it tests your ability to handle uncertainty like real-world engineering
- Strong candidates clarify requirements proactively before designing
- Cover five dimensions: core features, scale, non-functional requirements, constraints, and priorities
- Make assumptions explicitly, validate them, and show reasoning
- Good assumptions are reasonable, clearly stated, validated, and flexible
- Effective clarifying questions focus on scope, users, data, integration, and operations
- Use the 7-step approach: clarify, define scope, sketch, apply principles, communicate trade-offs, explain patterns, show restraint
- Proactive questioning demonstrates ownership and leadership
- Balance breadth (covering key dimensions) with depth (critical details)
- Validate understanding frequently throughout the interview
- Requirements gathering should take 5-10 minutes, not the whole interview
