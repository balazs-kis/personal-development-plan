# Lesson 4: Prioritization Frameworks for Technical Debt

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Apply systematic frameworks to prioritize technical debt
- Balance debt paydown with feature delivery
- Distinguish between high-value and low-value debt fixes
- Use the "interest rate" concept to identify urgent debt
- Implement time allocation strategies for debt work

---

## Introduction

With a debt inventory in hand and stakeholders on board, the question becomes: what do we fix first? Not all debt is equally urgent. Some debt causes daily pain and blocks progress; other debt sits dormant, causing little harm. Effective prioritization ensures limited time is spent on high-impact debt, not just the loudest complaints.

Good prioritization frameworks balance impact, cost, and strategic alignment, ensuring that debt paydown accelerates development rather than becoming an endless refactoring project.

## Key Concepts Summary

### The Prioritization Challenge

**The Problem:** Everything seems urgent. Every engineer has debt they want to fix. How do you choose?

**Key Questions:**
- What debt causes the most pain?
- What debt blocks the most work?
- What debt is easiest to fix?
- What debt has the highest ROI?

**Goal:** Maximize impact per unit of effort invested in debt paydown.

### Framework 1: The Three-Step Prioritization Framework

#### Step 1: Survey the Debt Landscape

**Map Your Systems:**
- Identify all components and how they relate to product workflows
- Understand dependencies and critical paths

**Identify Bottlenecks:**
- Where does development slow down?
- What consistently causes bugs or incidents?
- What blocks new features?

**Collaborative Discovery:**
- Involve representatives across teams
- Document pain points and recurring toil
- Get diverse perspectives on what hurts most

**Output:** Comprehensive map of technical debt across the system

#### Step 2: Calculate "Interest Rates"

For each piece of debt, assess:

**What happens if you do nothing?**
- Will it get worse? (Compounding)
- Will it stay the same? (Stable)
- Will it improve? (Self-healing, unlikely)

**How quickly does it degrade?**
- Rapidly (every change makes it worse)
- Slowly (occasional impact)
- Stable (not changing)

**How much current disruption?**
- Daily pain (blocks work constantly)
- Weekly pain (causes periodic issues)
- Rare pain (only in edge cases)

**Are you actively building on it?**
- Building new features on unstable foundations = High interest
- Maintaining dormant system = Lower interest

**Interest Rate Metaphor:**
```
High Interest Debt: 
- Active system with new development
- Rapidly degrading
- Causes daily disruption
- Getting worse with every change

Low Interest Debt:
- Dormant system in maintenance mode
- Stable over time
- Causes occasional issues
- Not blocking critical work
```

#### Step 3: Determine Activity and Impact

**Active Systems:**
- New features being built
- High traffic or business-critical
- Strategic importance
- **Priority:** Fix debt here first—it's actively costing you

**Dormant Systems:**
- Maintenance mode only
- Lower traffic or less critical
- Legacy but functional
- **Priority:** Can often defer or accept the debt

**Impact Assessment:**

| System Activity | Debt Impact | Priority |
|----------------|-------------|----------|
| Active, High Traffic | High | Critical - Fix Now |
| Active, Medium Traffic | Medium | High - Fix Soon |
| Dormant, High Criticality | Low-Medium | Medium - Monitor |
| Dormant, Low Criticality | Low | Low - Accept or Deprecate |

**Key Insight:** Pay down debt in active systems first. Debt in dormant systems is less urgent.

### Framework 2: The 25 Percent Rule (Shopify Model)

**Core Principle:** Allocate 25% of engineering time to technical debt across four categories:

#### 10% for Daily Debt (Tidying)

**Definition:** Small improvements made while working on regular features.

**Examples:**
- Extracting a method for clarity
- Renaming variables to be more descriptive
- Adding a missing test
- Refactoring a function you're already touching

**How to Implement:**
- Engineers should feel empowered to spend up to 4 hours/week improving code they're working in
- No permission needed—this is part of professional craftsmanship
- Should NOT be tracked as "debt work"—it's just doing the job well

**Culture:** Normalize refactoring as you go. "Leave the code better than you found it."

#### 10% for Weekly Debt (New Cards)

**Definition:** Debt that can be fixed within a sprint, added as cards/issues.

**Examples:**
- Extracting duplicated logic into a shared utility
- Adding tests for an untested module
- Updating deprecated dependencies
- Refactoring a complex function into smaller pieces

**How to Implement:**
- Add debt cards to sprint backlogs
- Allocate ~4 hours/week per team (16 hours/month for team of 4)
- One team member can take 1-2 days per sprint for these cards

**Key:** Don't always fix this immediately—sometimes it's better for someone else to gain momentum on it later.

#### 5% for Monthly and Yearly Debt (Planning)

**Definition:** Large debt projects requiring weeks to months, or strategic planning.

**Examples:**
- Major refactoring of a core system
- Rewriting a brittle service
- Migrating to a new architecture
- Modernizing a legacy codebase

**How to Implement:**
- Spend ~2 hours/week on planning and analysis
- Not about doing the work—about understanding the problem and deciding if/how/when to address it
- Includes backlog grooming, strategic planning meetings, architectural discussions

**Key:** This time is for thinking, not doing. Monthly/yearly debt requires dedicated team time and formal planning.

**Total Time:** 10% + 10% + 5% = 25% of engineering capacity allocated to debt

### Framework 3: Impact vs. Effort Matrix

Classic prioritization matrix with a debt-specific twist:

```
High Impact │ Do First      │ Plan Carefully
            │ (Quick Wins)  │ (Major Projects)
            │               │
────────────┼───────────────┼──────────────────
            │               │
Low Impact  │ Do Later      │ Avoid
            │ (Fill Time)   │ (Time Sinks)
            │               │
            └───────────────┴──────────────────
              Low Effort      High Effort
```

**Do First (High Impact, Low Effort):**
- Quick wins with big payoff
- Example: Remove unused dependencies, add logging

**Plan Carefully (High Impact, High Effort):**
- Major strategic projects
- Require dedicated time and planning
- Example: Rewrite authentication system

**Do Later (Low Impact, Low Effort):**
- Nice-to-haves that can fill spare time
- Example: Code formatting, minor refactors

**Avoid (Low Impact, High Effort):**
- Low ROI, high cost
- Question if it's worth doing at all
- Example: Perfect solution to edge-case issue

### Framework 4: Risk-Based Prioritization

Prioritize debt based on risk exposure:

**Critical Risk (Security, Compliance, Data Loss):**
- Example: Using unsupported library with known vulnerabilities
- **Priority:** Immediate

**High Risk (Frequent Production Incidents):**
- Example: Brittle deployment process causing outages
- **Priority:** Urgent (next sprint)

**Medium Risk (Development Slowdowns):**
- Example: Complex codebase causing bugs and slow features
- **Priority:** Important (next quarter)

**Low Risk (Quality of Life):**
- Example: Annoying but not blocking
- **Priority:** Defer or accept

### Framework 5: ROI-Based Prioritization

For each debt item, calculate:

```
ROI = (Annual Benefit) / (Cost to Fix)
```

**High ROI (>100%):** Fix it—pays for itself in less than a year

**Medium ROI (50-100%):** Good investment, prioritize if capacity allows

**Low ROI (<50%):** Questionable investment, consider deferring

**Example:**

| Debt Item | Cost | Annual Benefit | ROI | Priority |
|-----------|------|----------------|-----|----------|
| Fix auth duplication | 2 weeks | $60K saved | 300% | Critical |
| Migrate to new framework | 12 weeks | $80K saved | 67% | Medium |
| Refactor reports module | 4 weeks | $20K saved | 50% | Low |
| Clean up unused code | 1 week | $5K saved | 50% | Low |

### Balancing Debt with Features

**The Eternal Trade-off:** Features deliver value to customers; debt work enables future features.

**Strategies for Balance:**

**1. Dedicated Debt Time (25% Rule)**
- Allocate fixed percentage to debt
- No negotiation each sprint—it's built in

**2. Debt-to-Feature Ratio**
- For every N feature sprints, do 1 debt sprint
- Example: 3 feature sprints, 1 debt sprint

**3. Debt Tax**
- Every feature team must address related debt as part of the work
- Example: Adding a feature? Also refactor the module you're touching

**4. Debt Sprints / Cleanup Weeks**
- Periodic focused time (quarterly) where the entire team tackles debt
- Visible commitment to keeping systems healthy

**5. Opportunistic Refactoring**
- When touching code for a feature, improve it simultaneously
- Built into story estimates

**Which Strategy?**
- Small teams: 25% rule + opportunistic refactoring
- Large teams: Combination of dedicated debt time + periodic cleanup sprints
- Enterprise: Formal debt programs with dedicated teams

## Practice Exercise

After completing the materials, try this exercise (do not spend more than 20 minutes):

Take your debt inventory from Lesson 2. Prioritize the top 10 items:

1. **Calculate Interest Rates:** For each item, assess:
   - Is the system active or dormant?
   - Is the debt getting worse or stable?
   - How much daily/weekly disruption does it cause?

2. **Categorize by Timeframe:**
   - Daily debt (< 1 day to fix)
   - Weekly debt (1-5 days to fix)
   - Monthly debt (1-4 weeks to fix)
   - Yearly debt (months to fix)

3. **Calculate ROI (roughly):**
   - Cost to fix (engineer-weeks)
   - Annual benefit (time saved or revenue enabled)
   - ROI %

4. **Create Priority Order:**
   - Rank your top 5 items to fix first
   - Explain your reasoning

This exercise gives you hands-on practice with prioritization frameworks.
