# Lesson 2: Creating Alignment Across Teams

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Understand the concept of aligned autonomy and why it matters
- Use alignment maps to connect work to business outcomes
- Apply techniques to create technical alignment without authority
- Identify and address organizational barriers to alignment

---

## Introduction

Creating alignment is one of the most powerful forms of leadership without authority. When teams are aligned on direction and goals, they can move independently while still pulling in the same direction. This "aligned autonomy" enables scale—but it requires deliberate effort to establish and maintain.

As a Staff+ engineer, you often need to create alignment across teams where you have no direct authority. This lesson teaches you how.

## Key Concepts Summary

### Aligned Autonomy

**Definition:** Teams have freedom to make decisions independently while ensuring those decisions support broader company goals and strategic direction.

**The Balance:**
- **Too much autonomy:** Teams optimize locally, creating inconsistency and technical debt
- **Too little autonomy:** Teams wait for permission, slowing innovation and demotivating engineers
- **Aligned autonomy:** Teams understand the "why" and can make fast, consistent decisions

**Key Elements:**
1. **Clear strategic direction:** A north star that guides independent decisions
2. **Transparent communication:** Everyone understands organizational goals and constraints
3. **Trust-based culture:** Leadership trusts teams to determine implementation details
4. **Feedback loops:** Regular check-ins ensure alignment without micromanagement

### Alignment Maps

A visual tool that connects work to outcomes:

```
Business Outcome → IT Outcome → Initiative Owner → Initiatives
```

**Benefits:**
- Makes invisible alignment visible
- Identifies work that doesn't connect to outcomes
- Shows dependencies and owners
- Prevents teams from losing sight of objectives

**Best Practices:**
- Update monthly, not just at project start
- Display visibly in team areas
- Review regularly in team meetings
- Use to challenge work that lacks clear connection to outcomes

### Technical Vectors

The direction your organization's software changes over time:

**Example vectors:**
- Migrating from monolith to microservices
- Adopting observability practices
- Improving test coverage
- Standardizing on specific frameworks

**Aligning vectors across teams:**
1. **Fix hot spots:** Address immediate technical quality issues
2. **Adopt best practices:** Establish and communicate preferred approaches
3. **Identify leverage points:** Find places where small changes preserve quality at scale
4. **Create visibility:** Make technical direction clear through documentation and examples

### Overcoming Organizational Barriers

**Common antipatterns that block alignment:**
- **Silos:** Teams that don't collaborate or share information
- **Fiefdoms:** Teams that prioritize their own goals over organizational objectives
- **Conflicting incentives:** Reward structures that encourage misalignment

**Strategies to address:**
- Invest in inclusive decision-making processes
- Create cross-team working groups
- Share success stories across team boundaries
- Align metrics and incentives with collaboration

## Practice Exercise

After completing the materials, try this exercise (20 minutes):

**Create a Mini Alignment Map:**

1. **Choose a current initiative** you're involved in
2. **Work backwards:**
   - What IT outcome does it enable? (e.g., "Reduce deployment time")
   - What business outcome does that support? (e.g., "Faster time to market")
   - Who owns this initiative?
   - What other initiatives contribute to the same outcome?
3. **Identify gaps:**
   - Is the connection clear to all stakeholders?
   - Are there initiatives that don't connect to outcomes?
   - Are multiple teams working on overlapping solutions?

## Key Takeaways

- Aligned autonomy enables teams to move fast without constant coordination
- Clear strategic direction (the "why") is more valuable than detailed instructions (the "how")
- Alignment maps make invisible connections visible and help teams stay focused on outcomes
- Technical vectors provide consistent direction for how software evolves across teams
- Overcoming organizational barriers requires addressing incentive structures, not just communication
- Alignment is maintained through transparency and regular feedback loops, not control
